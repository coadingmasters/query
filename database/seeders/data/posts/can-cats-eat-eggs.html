Eggs are one of the more nutritious human foods a cat can safely eat, and that is not a coincidence: egg is already used as a protein source in some complete cat foods. Whole, cooked egg supplies protein a cat's body can use well, which is part of why a spoonful now and then shows up as an easy, low-effort treat in a lot of feeding routines. It is also cheap, quick to prepare, and already sitting in most kitchens, which is why the question comes up so often.

The question that actually matters is not whether eggs are safe in general, but how the egg in front of you was cooked. Raw and cooked eggs carry very different risks, and that one detail is what separates a genuinely useful treat from something worth skipping entirely. The rest of this guide works through that distinction, along with the smaller questions that tend to follow it: whether egg can replace a meal, what to do with the shell, and how much is actually reasonable to give.

This guide is written from published veterinary sources, named and linked as they come up and listed again at the bottom.

Are eggs safe for cats?

Yes, when the egg is cooked. Scrambled or hard-boiled, plain, and with nothing added, a cooked egg is a lean, easily digested protein that suits a cat's diet well and is genuinely nutritious in small amounts. Either method works; what matters is that the egg is cooked all the way through and served on its own.

Plain means exactly that. Leave out the butter, oil, salt, milk, and any onion or garlic seasoning that a scrambled egg might normally get for a person. A cat's portion should have nothing in it but the egg itself, cooked through and cooled to room temperature before it is offered. Almost the entire safety question with eggs comes down to preparation, not the egg as an ingredient, which is why the same food can be a fine treat or an unnecessary risk depending only on how it reaches the bowl.

There is no meaningful safety difference between scrambling an egg and hard-boiling it, as long as both are cooked plain and served without anything else mixed in. Pick whichever is easier: a small piece of hard-boiled egg white and yolk, or a spoonful of plain scrambled egg once it has cooled, both work the same way in a cat's bowl.

Can cats eat raw eggs?

No. Raw eggs are not worth the risk, even though a single raw egg is unlikely to cause immediate harm on its own.

The first concern is bacterial. Raw eggs carry a real risk of salmonella and other bacteria, the same concern that applies to feeding raw meat of any kind. An infection can make a cat unwell, and because it is a foodborne bacteria and not something specific to how a cat's body handles food, it can also spread to the people who handle the food or clean the litter box afterward. That second part is easy to overlook: the risk is not just to the cat eating the egg.

The second concern is nutritional rather than infectious. Raw egg white contains avidin, a protein that binds biotin, one of the B vitamins, and interferes with how much of it a cat's body can actually absorb from food. An occasional raw egg is not going to cause a deficiency on its own, since the effect depends on how often it happens rather than a single serving. Feeding raw eggs repeatedly over time is where the biotin risk actually builds, as less and less of the vitamin gets absorbed with each raw serving. Cooking denatures avidin and removes this problem entirely, which is the main reason cooked eggs are always the better choice over raw ones, for the biotin question as much as for the bacteria.

This is also the one part of feeding eggs where trends around raw diets for pets do not carry over cleanly. Whatever the reasoning behind feeding a cat raw meat, egg is a separate case with its own two-part risk, bacteria and biotin, and both of those are resolved simply by cooking the egg first. There is no upside to raw egg that cooking takes away.

Are eggs a complete meal on their own?

No. Egg is a good source of protein, but it is not a complete diet, and it should stay an occasional addition rather than a meal replacement.

It is missing taurine at the level and balance that a complete cat food is formulated to provide, the same gap that applies to plain chicken or any other single-ingredient food fed on its own. A cat that eats mostly egg, even cooked and prepared correctly, is still missing nutrients that a balanced commercial diet is built around and tested for. Eggs work well as a topper stirred into a complete diet, or as an occasional side, not as a stand-in for one, no matter how good the protein in an egg actually is.

This is worth spelling out because egg looks, on paper, like it should be enough: it is high in protein and easy for a cat to digest. What it cannot do is replicate the specific balance of nutrients a commercial cat food is formulated to hit, which is the actual bar for a complete diet rather than protein content alone. Keeping egg in the "extra" column rather than the "meal" column is the simplest way to avoid that gap.

Can cats eat eggshells?

There is no need to feed eggshell, though it is not toxic if a cat happens to eat a small piece by accident while investigating the kitchen counter.

Eggshell is sometimes suggested as a calcium source for pets, but there is no established benefit to feeding it to a cat, and it is not part of standard veterinary advice. Since it adds no real value and is not something a cat needs, the simpler approach is to stick to the egg white and yolk, cooked, and leave the shell out of the bowl altogether.

There is a difference between "not toxic" and "worth adding." A cat that licks a bit of shell residue off a plate is not in any danger, but that is not the same as a reason to crush shell into food on purpose. When the goal is a safe, simple treat, egg white and yolk already cover it without the shell being part of the plan.

Can cats be allergic to eggs?

It is possible, though a genuine egg allergy is less common in cats than allergies to chicken, fish, or beef, which are the food allergies seen more often in practice.

Signs of a real egg allergy or sensitivity include itchy skin, digestive upset, or ear infections that show up consistently after a cat eats egg, rather than a one-off reaction. If those signs appear every single time egg is on the menu, that pattern is worth taking seriously: stop feeding it and mention what you have noticed at the next vet visit, since a consistent pattern is what actually points to egg as the cause.

None of this means most cats need to avoid egg out of caution. Egg allergy is uncommon enough that it should not be the default assumption; it is simply worth knowing the signs so a real reaction is not mistaken for something else, or a coincidence mistaken for a pattern.

How much egg is safe?

A whole egg is a meaningful portion for an animal as small as a cat, and it is calorie-dense and moderately fatty enough that it should stay an occasional addition rather than a daily habit, even though the protein itself is a good one. A small piece of hard-boiled egg or a spoonful of plain scrambled egg, offered every so often rather than every day, is enough to count as a treat without turning into a habit that adds up.
