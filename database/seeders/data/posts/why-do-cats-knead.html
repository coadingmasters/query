If you have ever sat down with a cat on your lap and felt two paws pushing rhythmically into your leg, you have watched one of the oldest behaviors a cat has. It is called kneading, and most people know it better as making biscuits, because the motion looks like a baker working dough.

It looks odd, it sometimes hurts, and it almost always means something good. Here is what is actually happening.

Why do cats knead? The real reason

Kneading starts before a kitten can see. Newborns press their front paws against their mother's belly while nursing, and that pressure helps milk let down. It is one of the first things a kitten learns to do, and it is tied from the beginning to warmth, food and safety.

Most cats never let go of it. The behavior stops being about milk and becomes about the feeling that came with it. An adult cat kneading your lap is doing something closer to what a person does when they wrap their hands around a warm mug.

Kneading is not a request. It is a cat telling you, without meaning to, that nothing here needs guarding.

There are three things going on at once:

  • Comfort. The motion is self-soothing, which is why it so often comes with purring, half-closed eyes and a cat who has clearly stopped paying attention to the room.
  • Scent marking. Cats have scent glands in their paw pads. Every push leaves a trace of them on whatever they are kneading. It is not territorial in the way spraying is; it is closer to putting their name on something.
  • Nesting. Wild cats pat down grass and leaves before lying down. A cushion, a folded blanket or your thigh all trigger the same ancient check that the spot is soft enough.

Kittens start kneading within days of being born, before their eyes have even opened. It is one of the first things a cat ever learns to do.

Pummelling, padding, marching: same thing, different names

There is no agreed word for this, which is why searching for it is frustrating. Depending on where you are and who you ask, the same behavior is called:

  • Making biscuits, easily the most common online
  • Kneading, the term most vets and behaviorists use
  • Pummelling or pummelling the dough
  • Padding, marching or paddling
  • Pushing with their paws, which is what most people type before they know the word

All of them describe one behavior: alternating pressure from the front paws, usually slow, usually accompanied by purring. If you have seen your cat do it, you have seen all of the above.

Why does my cat knead me specifically?

Because kneading is a vulnerable thing to do, and cats do it where they feel least on guard. A cat mid-knead is not watching exits. If yours picks you over the sofa, that is a preference built on the fact that nothing bad has ever happened to them on your lap.

The scent marking matters here too. Cats build a shared group smell with the animals and people they live with, and kneading is one of the ways they top it up. Head-butting and rubbing along your shins do the same job.

If your own cat never kneads, nothing is wrong, and it is not a verdict on your relationship. Kneading is one of several ways cats show contentment. Slow blinking, sitting with their back to you, and simply choosing the room you are in all say the same thing.

Why does my cat knead blankets and soft fabric?

Soft, slightly yielding material is the closest thing in your house to a mother cat. Fleece blankets, wool jumpers, duvets and cushions all get the same response, and it is usually the most enthusiastic kneading you will see.

Some cats add suckling or drooling to it, and a few take a corner of the fabric into their mouth while their paws work. That combination looks strange but it is a normal part of the same package, especially in cats who were separated from their mother early. It is worth keeping an eye on only if the cat is swallowing fabric rather than mouthing it, which is a different behavior called pica and one to raise with your vet.

Why does my cat knead with claws out?

Because claws extend and retract as a natural part of the paw flexing, and most cats have no idea they are digging in. It is not aggression, and it is not your cat testing you.

Three things help, in order of how well they work:

  1. Keep the nails trimmed. A blunt claw on a lap is a completely different experience. Every two to three weeks is typical.
  2. Put a barrier down. Keep a folded blanket or a cushion where your cat likes to settle and move it onto your lap when they arrive.
  3. Redirect rather than reject. If it becomes too much, gently lift them onto the blanket beside you instead of pushing them off. Pushing a cat away mid-knead teaches them that settling on you ends badly.

What does not work is punishment of any kind. The cat is not doing anything wrong, and the only lesson available is that your lap is unpredictable.

Why is my kitten making biscuits on me?

Kittens knead more than adults, and more intensely, because for them the behavior is still fresh from nursing. A kitten making biscuits on your arm is running the same routine it ran on its mother a few weeks earlier.

It usually settles down as they mature, though plenty of cats keep it for life. If you have just brought a kitten home, it is one of the earliest signs that they have decided you are safe, and it tends to appear before they will sleep in the open near you.

When cats knead most

Whatever the trigger, it nearly always arrives with the same conditions:

  • Settling down, rather than mid-play
  • Being stroked, or lying against you
  • On something soft: a blanket, a cushion, a lap
  • Somewhere they are not watching the door

Every cat is different. Some knead daily, others a handful of times a year, and neither says anything about how attached they are.

Why do cats knead at night?

Evening is usually when the house goes quiet and the cat finally settles, and settling is exactly the state that brings kneading out. There is nothing nocturnal or strange about it.

Cats are crepuscular, which means they are naturally most active around dawn and dusk. A burst of energy in the evening followed by a long, kneading wind-down on your lap is the normal shape of a cat's day, not a sleep problem.

Should I be worried about kneading?

Almost never. Kneading itself is normal at every age and in every breed, and a cat that does it a lot is usually a cat that is comfortable.

What is worth attention is change. Cats hide discomfort extremely well, and a shift in any habit is often the first visible sign that something is wrong. Call your vet if kneading appears alongside:

Call your vet if kneading appears alongside any of the following:

  • Hiding, or a sudden drop in how much they seek you out
  • Eating noticeably more or less than usual
  • Grooming one area until the fur thins
  • Vocalising when picked up or touched in a particular spot
  • Any change in litter tray habits or how much they drink

The kneading is not the symptom in any of those. The change is. If you want a fuller list of what to watch for, our guide to the early signs a cat is unwell covers the ones that show up first.

None of it is a reason to worry on its own. Kneading is one of the more reassuring things a cat does, and the only version of it worth watching for is a sudden change from whatever is normal for yours.
