Cats sneeze for the same reason people do: something tickled the inside of their nose. Dust from the litter box, a gust of cold air, a strong perfume, and it is over in a second and forgotten. That is most sneezing, and it needs nothing from you.

What is worth paying attention to is the pattern around it. A single sneeze from an otherwise normal cat is not the same thing as sneezing that keeps happening, or that arrives with a runny nose, watery eyes or a cat that has stopped eating. Here is how to tell the difference.

This guide is written from published veterinary sources, named and linked as they come up and listed again at the bottom. It is not an examination of your own cat, and nothing here replaces one.

Is it normal for a cat to sneeze?

Yes, occasionally. A cat's nose is at floor level and close to whatever it is investigating, so it picks up more dust, pollen and fine litter particles than yours does. One or two sneezes while your cat is otherwise playing, eating and behaving normally is not a sign of anything.

What changes the picture is frequency and company. Sneezing that repeats through the day, that continues for more than a day or two, or that arrives alongside discharge, is no longer just dust.

When should I take my sneezing cat to the vet?

This is the section worth reading first if you are actually worried right now. Call the vet if any of the following apply:

  • Sneezing has continued for more than two or three days
  • There is any blood in the sneeze or the discharge
  • Discharge has turned yellow or green
  • Only one nostril is affected
  • Your cat has stopped eating, or seems unusually quiet or lethargic
  • Breathing looks labored, or your cat is breathing through their mouth
  • It is a kitten, a senior cat, or a cat with an existing health condition

None of these need to wait for a routine appointment. A same-day or next-day visit is the right call for any of them, and blood in a sneeze specifically is worth calling ahead about rather than just booking in.

Why does my cat keep sneezing?

Sneezing that keeps coming back usually means one of two things: an ongoing irritant, or an infection.

An irritant is the easier one to rule out. Scented candles, air fresheners, cleaning sprays, cigarette smoke and dusty, scented litter are the most common causes, and switching the litter or airing the room for a day is often enough to tell you whether that was it.

If nothing has changed in the house, an upper respiratory infection is the more likely explanation. Feline herpesvirus and calicivirus are extremely common in cats, particularly cats who spent time in a shelter or came from a large litter, and the Cornell Feline Health Center lists both among the most frequent causes of cold-like symptoms in cats, sneezing included.

My cat is sneezing but acting completely normal. Should I worry?

Generally, no. A cat that is still eating, drinking, playing and seeking you out despite a mild sneeze is not showing signs of a serious problem. The behavior around the sneezing tells you more than the sneezing itself.

Keep an eye on it rather than dismissing it. Mild viral infections sometimes start exactly this way, with a few days of sneezing and nothing else, before other symptoms appear or before it resolves on its own. If mild sneezing continues for several days, even while your cat seems otherwise well, it is worth a call to your vet for advice rather than waiting it out. Symptoms vary enough by cause that "wait and see" is not a rule you can rely on from a distance.

My cat is sneezing and has a runny nose or watery eyes

This combination is one of the clearest signs of an upper respiratory infection. What the discharge looks like is a useful clue, though not a reliable one on its own:

  • Clear discharge tends to go with the early, viral stage of an infection, the feline equivalent of a common cold.
  • Thick, yellow or green discharge can mean a secondary bacterial infection has joined the virus. Color is a clue a vet weighs alongside everything else, not proof on its own, and whether antibiotics are actually needed is their call to make, not something to decide from a photo or a description.
  • Discharge from one nostril only is different again, and points more toward something physically stuck in the nose, a tooth root problem, or in older cats, a nasal polyp.

Watery eyes alongside sneezing is common with the same viral infections, since feline herpesvirus affects the eyes as often as the nose.

Why is my kitten sneezing so much?

Kittens catch upper respiratory infections easily, and their symptoms tend to hit harder than an adult cat's. A kitten's immune system has not built up the same resistance yet, which is why sneezing, watery eyes and congestion spread quickly through shelters and multi-kitten litters.

Take a kitten who stops eating more seriously than you would an adult with the same symptoms. A blocked nose can be enough to put a kitten off food because they cannot smell it, and kittens dehydrate and lose weight faster than adult cats do. A kitten that stops eating is worth a call to the vet, especially if it is also congested, lethargic, or has eye or nasal discharge alongside it, rather than something to time by the hour.

What causes a cat to sneeze?

According to VCA Animal Hospitals, roughly in order of how often each one turns out to be the cause:

  1. Viral upper respiratory infection. Feline herpesvirus and calicivirus, by far the most common cause of repeated sneezing.
  2. Environmental irritants. Dust, scented litter, cleaning products, smoke and strong perfume.
  3. Allergies. Less common in cats than in people, but they do happen, usually alongside itchy skin or watery eyes.
  4. A foreign body. A blade of grass or a seed caught in the nose causes sudden, intense, usually one-sided sneezing.
  5. Dental disease. A tooth root abscess near the nasal passage can produce sneezing and discharge that looks respiratory but is not.
  6. Nasal polyps or, rarely, a tumor. Usually in older cats, and usually chronic rather than sudden, with sneezing that persists for weeks.

A vet works through this list roughly in this order too, which is part of why a physical exam matters more than guessing from symptoms alone.

Can I do anything at home while I wait?

A little, and only as comfort rather than treatment. A humidifier, or a few minutes sitting with your cat in a steamy bathroom, can loosen congestion and make breathing easier. Gently wiping discharge away from the nose and eyes with a damp cloth keeps your cat more comfortable and stops it crusting.

What not to do is reach for a human cold remedy. Decongestants, antihistamines and pain relievers made for people are frequently unsafe for cats, and several common ones are outright toxic. If your cat needs medication, it needs to come from a vet who has actually examined them.
