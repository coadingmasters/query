An occasional hairball is one of the more normal, if unglamorous, parts of owning a cat. But "occasional" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Hairballs that show up every week, or that turn into repeated retching with nothing coming up, are not just a grooming side effect anymore. They are a sign worth paying attention to.

Why do cats get hairballs in the first place?

A cat's tongue is covered in small, backward-facing hooks called papillae, which work as a built-in comb every time they groom. Most of the loose fur that comb picks up gets swallowed, and most of that fur passes straight through the digestive tract and out in the litter box without anyone noticing.

Some of it does not. Fur that lingers in the stomach instead of moving on can clump together into a compact, usually cylindrical mass, a trichobezoar, which is the technical name for what everyone just calls a hairball. When the stomach cannot move it along, the cat's body does the only other thing it can: bring it back up.

A hairball is not a failure of grooming. It is what grooming looks like when it works exactly as designed, most of the time.

How often is normal?

For most short-haired, healthy adult cats, a hairball every few weeks or less is unremarkable. Cats that groom heavily, shed a lot, or have long coats bring one up more often, simply because there is more loose fur in circulation to begin with.

Frequency worth paying attention to looks more like this:

  • Long-haired breeds (Persians, Maine Coons, Ragdolls) may reasonably produce one every couple of weeks during heavy shedding seasons.
  • Short-haired cats bringing one up weekly or more often are grooming more than expected, which is worth a look on its own.
  • Any cat retching repeatedly without producing anything is not a hairball problem at all; it is a different symptom that needs its own answer.

When frequent hairballs point at something else

A cat that suddenly starts producing hairballs far more often than before is usually grooming more, not just shedding more, and grooming that increases sharply has its own list of causes:

  • Stress or anxiety. Cats often over-groom as a self-soothing behavior in response to a change at home, a new pet, or conflict with another cat.
  • Skin irritation or allergies. Fleas, food sensitivities and environmental allergies all cause itching that leads to more licking and more swallowed fur.
  • Pain in a specific area. A cat that repeatedly over-grooms one spot, rather than all over, is often licking at something that hurts underneath, not something itchy on the surface.

Bald or thinning patches, especially on the belly or inner legs, are the clearest sign that grooming has crossed from normal into a symptom.

What actually reduces hairballs

Most of the useful fixes work on the same idea: less loose fur available to swallow, and an easier path through the gut for what is swallowed.

  1. Brush regularly. A few minutes a few times a week removes loose fur before your cat does, and is the single most effective thing most owners can do. Long-haired cats benefit from daily brushing, especially during shedding season.
  2. Consider a hairball-control diet. Foods formulated for hairball control add extra fiber, which helps carry swallowed fur through the digestive tract rather than letting it sit and clump.
  3. Keep water intake up. Good hydration supports normal gut motility. Wet food contributes meaningfully more water than dry kibble alone.
  4. Ask about a hairball remedy. Petroleum- or oil-based lubricant pastes, sold specifically for cats, can help fur pass through rather than accumulate. Check with a vet before starting one regularly, since overuse can interfere with nutrient absorption.

When a hairball is actually an emergency

A true intestinal blockage from a hairball is uncommon, but it happens, and it is a genuine emergency when it does. The warning signs look different from an ordinary hairball:

  • Repeated retching or gagging with nothing produced
  • Vomiting that continues over more than a day
  • Loss of appetite lasting more than 24 hours
  • Lethargy, or a visibly swollen or painful abdomen
  • Constipation or straining without a bowel movement

Any of those, especially in combination, is a same-day call to the vet rather than something to wait out. A blocked intestine can become life-threatening quickly and sometimes needs surgery to resolve.
